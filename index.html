Is a Linear Probe Evidence of a Linear Representation?

1Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory, 2Bielefeld University
*Equal contribution

Abstract

The linear representation hypothesis holds that pretrained vision models encode generative factors as linearly decodable directions in feature space, and comes with a standard methodology: train a linear probe, declare a factor represented if accuracy is high. We show this test is too permissive. Asymptotically, any sufficiently rich feature map, including a random one, admits a linear readout, so high probe accuracy is consistent with two very different geometries: a true linear world model, where the latent-aligned subspace dominates the representation, or a kernel machine, where the latents lie linearly inside a much larger curved manifold. This distinction matters because the curved structure orthogonal to the probe is relevant for out-of-distribution generalization, robustness, and compositional transfer. We propose three asymmetric diagnostics that distinguish the two cases: reverse predictivity, spectral concentration, and out-of-distribution generalization. We validate them in a fully observed simulation where ground truth is known. Applied to six pretrained vision transformers on SVG-World, our deterministic generative benchmark with paired visual styles, and to two of them on CelebA, they show pretraining produces structure beyond a random kernel but well short of a linear world model. On dSprites, 3D Shapes and MPI3D, forward decodability and latent-aligned variance dissociate. Thus, forward decoding alone does not show that the probed factors align with the dominant geometry of the representation. In summary, our work cautions about the success of linear probes as opposed to truly linear and low dimensional representations.

TL;DR: A high linear-probe score does not show that a model has a linear world model: with enough features, even a random network is linearly decodable. Three asymmetric diagnostics, reverse predictivity, spectral concentration and out-of-distribution transfer, tell the two apart. Across six vision transformers and on real photographs, pretraining adds latent-aligned structure well above random initialization, but far short of a linear world model.

The Question

Linear World Model vs. Kernel Machine. (a) 2D latent grid. (b, c) Cartoons: a linear world model lifts the grid to a flat plane (Def. 1); a kernel-machine map might lift it to a curved surface. Forward $R^2$ is high in both; reverse $R^2$ separates them (Prop. 2). (d, e) Empirical realization at $h = 1024$: horizontal axes are forward-decoded $\hat z_1, \hat z_2$; vertical is the top variance direction in the decoder's null space, residualized against $\hat z$. Trained is a plane, random a curved manifold.

The standard inference. Train a linear probe from frozen features to the generative latents and declare a latent “represented” when $R^2$ is high. The implicit claim: if $z$ is linearly decodable from $f(x)$, then $f$ has learned $z$ as an axis of its representation. We formalize the strongest version of this claim, namely that $z$ spans the dominant directions of $f$'s variance, as a linear world model of $z$.

Why it is too permissive. Rich nonlinear feature maps, including random ones, are universal function approximators, so almost any sufficiently smooth target can be linearly read out from them. A high forward $R^2$ is therefore consistent with two distinct hypotheses: (H1) $f$ is a linear world model of $z$, or (H2) the model is a high-dimensional feature map in which $z$ lies linearly but carries a small share of the variance.

Theoretical Results

Definition 1: Linear World Model
$f$ implements a linear world model of $z$ with residual level $\sigma^2 \ge 0$ if, with $\tilde z = \Cov(z)^{-1/2}(z - \E[z])$ the whitened latents, there exist $W \in \R^{d \times k}$ and $\mu \in \R^d$ such that $$f(x) = W \tilde z(x) + \mu + \varepsilon(x), \qquad \E\big[\varepsilon(x)\, \tilde z(x)^\top\big] = 0, \qquad \E\big[\|\varepsilon(x)\|^2\big] \le \sigma^2 \|W\|_F^2.$$ Since $\Cov(\tilde z) = I$, $\|W\|_F^2$ is the variance of $f$ carried by the latents, so $\sigma^2$ is the ratio of residual to latent-aligned variance. This is strictly stronger than “$z$ is linearly decodable from $f$”: it requires that most of $f$'s variance is aligned with $z$, not merely that $z$ lies inside the span of $f$.
Proposition 1: Forward Decodability Is Asymptotically Free
Let $\kappa$ be a bounded continuous kernel whose RKHS contains every coordinate $z_j$ of the ground-truth latents, and let $f_{\text{rand}}(x) \in \R^d$ be a random feature map such that $f_{\text{rand}}(x)^\top f_{\text{rand}}(x') \to \kappa(x, x')$ as $d \to \infty$. Then for any $\epsilon > 0$ and $\delta \in (0, 1)$ there is a $d_{\epsilon,\delta}$ such that for all $d \ge d_{\epsilon,\delta}$, with probability at least $1 - \delta$ over the draw of $f_{\text{rand}}$, some linear readout achieves $\E_x \|W f_{\text{rand}}(x) - z(x)\|^2 \le \epsilon$. In the limit, forward linear decodability is a property of having enough random features rather than of having learned anything about $z$.
Proposition 2: Reverse-Predictivity Asymmetry
Suppose $f(x) = A z(x) + \mu + \varepsilon(x)$ with $\E[\varepsilon z^\top] = 0$. Then $$R^2_{\rev} = \frac{\tr\!\big(A \Cov(z) A^\top\big)}{\tr\!\big(A \Cov(z) A^\top\big) + \tr \Cov(\varepsilon)}.$$ In particular, if $\varepsilon$ is dominated by a high-dimensional $z$-uncorrelated nonlinear component (the kernel-machine regime), $R^2_{\rev}$ is small even when forward $R^2 \approx 1$.
Proposition 3: Ceiling
For any $f$, let $\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d$ be the eigenvalues of $\Cov(f)$ and $r = \operatorname{rank} \Cov(z)$. Then $$R^2_{\rev} \;\le\; C_r = \frac{\sum_{i \le r} \lambda_i}{\sum_{i \le d} \lambda_i},$$ with equality iff the range of $W_{\rev}$ lies in a leading $r$-dimensional principal subspace of $f$ and the reverse-probe residual has no variance in that range. The ratio $R^2_{\rev} / C_r$ makes intermediate values interpretable: it is the fraction of the attainable share that the factors take, comparable across encoders with different spectra.

Three Asymmetric Diagnostics

Each test exploits a different asymmetry between Definition 1 and the mere existence of a linear decoder.

(D1) Reverse Predictivity
Under Definition 1 the latent-aligned subspace accounts for a large share of feature variance. We therefore predict the features from the latents: $$R^2_{\rev} = 1 - \frac{\E_x \|f(x) - W_{\rev} z(x) - \mu\|^2}{\E_x \|f(x) - \E f\|^2}, \qquad W_{\rev} = \arg\min_{W, \mu} \E_x \|f(x) - W z(x) - \mu\|^2.$$
(D2) Spectral Concentration
Under Definition 1 with small $\sigma^2$, the latent-aligned subspace is spanned by the leading principal directions of $f$. Project $f$ onto its top $m$ principal components, rescale each to unit variance, and let $R^2_{\rev}(m)$ be the reverse $R^2$ of the result. We report $\mathrm{SC} = R^2_{\rev}(r)$: the extent to which the $r$ leading directions of $f$ are linear functions of the latents. A strict linear world model has $\mathrm{SC} \approx 1$; a kernel machine, whose leading directions are dominated by $z$-uncorrelated variation, has low SC even when forward $R^2$ is high.
(D3) Out-of-Distribution Generalization
Linear decodability that holds only within the training support is consistent with overfitting a rich nonlinear kernel on that support. We train linear probes on one visual-style condition and evaluate them on another with the same latent layout: an encoder that has learned position as a factor, rather than as a style-conditional pattern, should transfer.

For a linear world model with small $\sigma^2$, all three diagnostics are close to their maximum. For a rich kernel-machine map, forward $R^2$ can be high while all three are low. The diagnostics are therefore necessary conditions: low scores rule out a linear world model of $z$, high scores do not establish one.

Empirical Validation

A Fully Observed Simulation

Setup. Latents $z \sim \mathrm{Unif}([-1, 1]^2)$ are first lifted by a frozen random Fourier map to 256 dimensions, then mapped to features $f \in \R^h$ by a two-layer ReLU MLP. We compare two regimes at matched hidden width $h$: random init, fresh weights and no training; and trained, the same architecture trained end-to-end with a linear readout to recover $z$, with weight decay $\lambda \in \{0, 1\}$. The trained network is nonlinear: at $h = 1024$ it visits 1988 ReLU activation regions on average and its Jacobian varies across inputs.

Forward decoding alone does not separate the two. Random-init forward $R^2$ rises smoothly with width and reaches $\approx 1$ by $h \approx 100$, as Proposition 1 predicts. Reverse $R^2$ keeps them apart at every width. At $h = 1024$, reverse $R^2$ is 0.9935 with $\lambda = 1$ vs. 0.7841 with $\lambda = 0$, at identical forward $R^2$ of 1.000: satisfying Definition 1 is a tunable property of training pressure, not just a binary trained-vs-not property.

Diagnostics on the simulation across hidden width $h$. Random init (orange) reproduces the asymptotic random-features prediction: forward $R^2 \to 1$ as $h$ grows, while reverse $R^2$ and the top-2 share stay low and OOD $R^2$ catches up only at large widths. Trained with weight decay $\lambda = 1$ (dark blue), all four diagnostics sit flat at $\approx 1$ across widths. Without weight decay (light blue), reverse $R^2$ and spectral concentration dip at intermediate widths: the linear-world-model condition is sensitive to training pressure even when forward $R^2$ saturates.

The spectral picture. The trained covariance is nearly rank two: the top two components carry 99.57% of the variance, and the projection onto them is an axis-aligned image of the latent square. The random model's spectrum decays smoothly over hundreds of components, and its top-two projection folds the latent square onto itself.

Spectral diagnostics on the simulation ($h = 1024$; random init vs. trained with $\lambda = 1$). (a, b) Test points on the top-2 PCs of $f$, colored by $z_0$: random folds the latent square into a non-axis-aligned shape; trained gives an axis-aligned image. (c) Normalized eigenspectrum of $f$: trained drops by several orders of magnitude after PC 2, random decays smoothly over hundreds of PCs. (d) Reverse $R^2$ on the top $m$ PCs of $f$, without whitening: trained stays at 1, random falls from $\approx 0.7$ at $m = 1$ to a plateau at its full $R^2_{\rev} \approx 0.2$.

SVG-World

To test pretrained vision models on the same terms, we need a data source with a small set of continuous latents, deterministic rendering, enough visual richness for nontrivial features, multiple objects, and a paired second visual style with the same latent layout. Each scene is rendered from a 32-dim latent vector with 20 active dimensions: four people with an $(x, z)$ position, a facing direction and a skin hue, and two animals with an $(x, z)$ position. The layout is identical in AquaWorld and WestWorld; only the rendering differs ($N = 10{,}000$ scenes each, $224 \times 224$).

SVG-World, a deterministic two-style benchmark with a shared latent layout. Both panels show the canonical scene at $z = 0$; arrows mark each object's two position latents ($+x$ horizontal, $+z$ depth). AquaWorld and WestWorld share the same 32-dim layout but differ in rendering, which enables the cross-domain transfer test (D3).

DINOv3 forward decoding. We extract frozen DINOv3-B/16 features on both worlds (CLS token and mean of patch tokens, $d = 768$), fit ridge probes per latent, and compare against random-init DINOv3, the same architecture with freshly initialized weights. Pretrained DINOv3 recovers all 12 position latents with mean $\rho \approx 0.94$ ($R^2 = 0.88$) on CLS and 0.91 (0.82) on mean-pooled patches, the kind of evidence routinely cited for the linear representation hypothesis. Random init partially decodes person positions ($R^2 = 0.41$) but loses the animals almost completely ($R^2 = 0.17$). This forward gap does not by itself establish Definition 1; the asymmetric diagnostics test the structural claim directly.

Forward decodability separates pretrained from random init at $d = 768$. Per-latent $\rho$ on CLS features. Pretrained averages $\rho = 0.94$ ($R^2 = 0.88$); random averages 0.55 (0.33), a gap of $\Delta\rho = 0.38$ that rises to 0.58 on the animals.

Reverse predictivity and spectral concentration. On raw CLS features, pretrained DINOv3 reaches $R^2_{\rev} = 0.18$ vs. 0.02 for random init; on mean-pooled patches, 0.28 vs. 0.02. Over five random initializations, the CLS gap is 0.187 [0.182, 0.191] in AquaWorld and 0.143 [0.139, 0.148] in WestWorld. The pretrained-vs-random ratio is about $10\times$, larger than the forward ratio of $\sim 1.7\times$: the forward gap understates how much more latent-aligned variance pretraining has. Against the ceiling of Proposition 3 the separation is sevenfold: on AquaWorld CLS features ($R^2_{\rev} = 0.212$, $C_{12} = 0.609$), pretrained reaches 0.35 of the attainable share and random init 0.05. Pretrained DINOv3-B/16 needs its top 17 principal components to collect 90% of its latent-predictable variance, against 55 to 59 for its random initializations.

Reverse predictivity (D1) and spectral concentration (D2) on SVG-World. $R^2_{\rev}$ vs. PCA-whitening cutoff $k$. Solid: top-$k$ PCs whitened to unit variance. Dashed: raw features. Pretrained's latent-aligned variance concentrates in the top $\sim$32 to 64 PCs; random's is diffuse. The curve falls with $k$ for any representation; what separates the two is its value at small $k$.

Out-of-distribution generalization across visual styles. We train a ridge probe on one world's features and evaluate it on the other's, with features mean-centered per world so that what is tested is the transfer of directions, not feature means. Pretrained averages $\rho = 0.91$ within domain vs. 0.51 for random ($\Delta\rho = 0.41$). Cross-domain both fall (pretrained 0.65, random 0.23), but the gap is unchanged: $\Delta\rho = 0.42$. The encoder's positional directions transfer across the AquaWorld↔WestWorld stylistic gap as cleanly as they exist within a single world. We report $\rho$ because feature scales differ between the two styles; after rescaling the probe output on half of the target style, transfer $R^2$ is 0.16 and 0.31 in the two directions for pretrained, against 0.01 and 0.02 for random init.

Asymmetric diagnostic D3: cross-domain transfer. Per-latent test $\rho$ on DINOv3-B/16 mean-pooled patch features; cross-domain values average both transfer directions. The pretrained-vs-random gap survives the style change ($\Delta\rho = 0.41$ within, 0.42 across domains). Animal positions transfer best (pretrained $\rho = 0.68$ to 0.84 cross-domain; random near zero).

Beyond SVG-World

Six vision transformers. We compare self-distillation (DINOv3 S/16, B/16, L/16; DINOv2-B/14), supervised classification (DeiT-B/16) and image-text contrastive learning (CLIP-B/16), each against a random initialization of the same architecture. All six have higher $R^2_{\rev}$ than their random initializations, by 0.145 to 0.235, with 95% intervals above zero. Within DINOv3, larger models decode better but carry less latent-aligned variance: from S/16 to L/16, forward $R^2$ rises from 0.855 to 0.914 while $R^2_{\rev}$ falls from 0.257 to 0.169.

Controlled benchmarks. On dSprites and MPI3D-realistic, the pretrained encoder decodes the factors better than its random initialization (forward $R^2$ 0.42 vs. 0.16 and 0.85 vs. 0.61) but has a lower $R^2_{\rev}$; on 3D Shapes forward $R^2$ is tied. The random initializations on these datasets are nearly low-dimensional (participation ratio at most 3.8, against 8.7 to 16.6 pretrained), so most of their variance lies in the top $r$ components and their ceiling is close to 1. The comparisons also depend on how the factors are encoded: four of the six encoder-benchmark pairs keep the sign of the gap under all three encodings, two do not. A reverse-predictivity comparison is therefore defined only for a fully specified factor set and its encoding, and should not be read as a score of encoder quality.

Real photographs. On CelebA, with the 40 annotated attributes as the factor set, the pretrained encoders reach 0.28 to 0.36 of their ceiling against 0.07 to 0.11 at random initialization, close to the SVG-World values (0.35 vs. 0.05). Spectral concentration separates them as clearly (0.15 to 0.17 vs. 0.02 to 0.03). CLIP's raw $R^2_{\rev}$ exceeds that of DINOv3 CLS (0.192 vs. 0.170), but both reach 0.28 of the ceiling.

In summary, pretraining raises the share of latent-aligned variance well above random initialization on every encoder and dataset where the comparison is unambiguous, but the probed factors take at most about half of what any linear image of them could reach.

What this means for probe-based claims. A successful linear probe is compatible with two quite different representations: one whose main directions of variance are affine in the latents, and one in which the latents are a single linear readout among many from a rich nonlinear expansion of the input. Pretrained encoders fit neither picture: they carry far more latent-aligned variance than their random initializations, in the leading principal directions, yet the probed factors take only part of what a linear image of them could reach. We therefore suggest reporting, beside probe accuracy, reverse predictivity against its ceiling, where the latent-aligned variance sits in the spectrum, and transfer across conditions.
